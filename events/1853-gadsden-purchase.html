The Gadsden Purchase (Venta de La Mesilla)

December 30, 1853 Purchase Treaty between the United States of America and the Mexican Republic (10 Stat. 1031)

Executive Summary

The U.S. purchased 29,670 square miles in southern Arizona and New Mexico for $10 million to secure an all-weather southern transcontinental railroad route.

Historical Narrative & Geopolitical Significance

In the early 1850s, American railroad promoters and southern politicians (notably Secretary of War Jefferson Davis) sought a viable low-elevation, snow-free transcontinental railroad corridor south of the Gila River to connect New Orleans and Texas to San Diego. Boundary disputes resulting from the flawed Disturnell map in the 1848 treaty, along with Mexican demands for reparations regarding cross-border Native raids under Article XI, threatened renewed hostilities. President Franklin Pierce dispatched rail promoter James Gadsden to negotiate with Mexican President Antonio López de Santa Anna. Finalized in December 1853 and ratified in amended form in June 1854, the treaty transferred 29,670 square miles of desert terrain in the Mesilla Valley for $10 million. This acquisition established the final, permanent international border of the contiguous 48 states.

Indigenous Sovereignty & Homelands Context

The Mesilla Valley and Sonoran borderlands were the sovereign territories of the Tohono O'odham, Akimel O'odham (Pima), Chiricahua Apache, and Western Apache. The new international border split traditional Tohono O'odham homelands and sacred pilgrimage routes in half, imposing foreign customs and military checkpoints across families and communities that persist to this day.

Boundary Definition & Cartographic Precision

Rigorous astronomical and geodetic joint boundary commission survey headed by Major William H. Emory (U.S.) and Don José Salazar Ylarregui (Mexico), marked by stone monuments along the 31°20' N and 31°47' N parallels.

Modern States Formed or Overlapping

This territorial acquisition includes all or parts of the following modern U.S. states: AZ, NM.
