TABULA XII · ANNO MDCCCXCVIII

Annexation of the Hawaiian Islands

July 7, 1898 Annexation Joint Resolution to Provide for Annexing the Hawaiian Islands (Newlands Resolution; 30 Stat. 750)

Executive Summary

The United States annexed the Republic of Hawaii by joint resolution of Congress amidst the Spanish-American War, securing strategic mid-Pacific naval position.

Historical Narrative & Geopolitical Significance

In January 1893, a coalition of American and European sugar planters and businessmen, backed by armed U.S. Marines dispatched by Minister John L. Stevens, executed a coup d'état overthrowing Queen Liliʻuokalani and the constitutional monarchy of the Hawaiian Kingdom. An investigation commissioned by President Grover Cleveland (the Blount Report) concluded the overthrow was an act of war and illegal under international law, and Cleveland refused to annex the islands. The oligarchic leaders established the Republic of Hawaii and waited for a favorable administration. During the Spanish-American War of 1898, as Admiral George Dewey's fleet engaged Spanish forces in Manila Bay (Philippines), expansionist leaders argued Hawaii was militarily indispensable as a Pacific coaling station. Unable to achieve the required two-thirds treaty majority in the Senate, Congress pushed through annexation via the Newlands Resolution by a simple majority vote on July 7, 1898.

Indigenous Sovereignty & Homelands Context

Over 21,000 Native Hawaiians (Kanaka Maoli)—representing more than half the adult Native population—signed the 1897 Kūʻē Petitions (Anti-Annexation Petitions), traveling across the islands to protest the loss of their independent nation and sovereign monarch. In 1993, the U.S. Congress passed the Apology Resolution (Public Law 103-150), officially acknowledging that the Hawaiian Kingdom was overthrown without the consent of the Native Hawaiian people and that their inherent sovereignty was never relinquished.

Boundary Definition & Cartographic Precision

The Hawaiian archipelago comprises eight major islands (Hawaiʻi, Maui, Oʻahu, Kauaʻi, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Niʻihau, Kahoʻolawe) and numerous northwestern atolls, spanning ~6,423 square miles in the central Pacific.

Modern States Formed or Overlapping

This territorial acquisition includes all or parts of the following modern U.S. states: HI.
