TABULA IX · ANNO MDCCCXLVIII

Mexican Cession (Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo)

February 2, 1848 Treaty Treaty of Peace, Friendship, Limits, and Settlement with the Republic of Mexico

Executive Summary

Ended the Mexican-American War. Mexico ceded ~525,000 square miles (55% of its territory) including California and the American Southwest for $15 million.

Historical Narrative & Geopolitical Significance

The Mexican-American War (1846–1848) concluded following the capture of Mexico City by General Winfield Scott. Signed at the Basilica of Guadalupe in Villa de Guadalupe Hidalgo by American diplomat Nicholas Trist and Mexican plenipotentiaries, the treaty established the Rio Grande as the boundary of Texas and ceded Alta California and Santa Fe de Nuevo México to the United States. This Mexican Cession encompassed present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming. In return, the United States paid Mexico $15 million in cash and assumed up to $3.25 million of Mexican government debts owed to U.S. citizens. Gold was discovered at Sutter's Mill in California just days before the treaty was signed, triggering the 1849 Gold Rush and catapulting California into immediate statehood in 1850.

Indigenous Sovereignty & Homelands Context

The cession treated ancestral homelands of hundreds of sovereign Indigenous nations—including the Navajo (Diné), Apache, Hopi, Zuni, Ute, Paiute, Shoshone, Pima, Tohono O'odham, and diverse California tribes—as transferred real estate. California's statehood unleashed an officially sanctioned campaign of extermination, forced servitude, and bounty-hunting against Native Californians, reducing the Indigenous population from an estimated 150,000 in 1848 to under 30,000 by 1870 (widely documented by historians as the California Genocide).

Boundary Definition & Cartographic Precision

The boundary followed the Rio Grande to the southern border of New Mexico, westward to the Gila River, down the Gila to the Colorado River, and along a straight surveyed line westward to the Pacific Ocean south of San Diego. Based on J. Disturnell's 1847 Map of the United Mexican States, which contained substantial positional inaccuracies for the town of Paso del Norte (El Paso).

Modern States Formed or Overlapping

This territorial acquisition includes all or parts of the following modern U.S. states: CA, NV, UT, AZ, NM, CO, WY.
